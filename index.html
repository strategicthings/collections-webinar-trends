AccountsRecovery.net programming archive · 2021–2026

I tested four theories about collections messaging. One cleanly held.

1,303 webinar sessions from one trade publication's archive, scored against the hypothesis that the conversation went portals, then texting, then "nobody answers the phone," then voice bots. One part holds, one is plainly wrong, one I cannot test with this data, and the one that looked most interesting did not survive its own robustness check.

1,303 dated sessions 2021-10-22 to 2026-09-15 20 quarters titles only, bodies paywalled

Corrected three times on 2026-09-16. Three rounds of independent code review. The classifier was rebuilt, six duplicate events removed, one verdict downgraded to "not established," and the sponsorship finding withdrawn entirely once its sample was rebuilt against the current classifier. Every number below is the corrected one. Full list in section 08.

02

One line does something the others don't

Share of each quarter's sessions mentioning the theme

AI went from 0.8% of 2022 programming to 19.8% of 2026. One session in five. Nothing else in the archive moves like that.

The channel themes behave the way industry arguments usually do. They spike while the question is live and fade once it is settled. Texting and omnichannel peaked at 9.4% in 2024 and have declined for two years. Digital-first strategy peaked earlier still, in 2023.

04

The question about humans flipped

14 sessions, 2022 to 2026, read in order

The "human" thread is too small to chart, but it is the most legible thing in the corpus when you read it chronologically. From 2022 through 2025 these sessions asked whether the human could be removed: Where Tech Can Replace Humans (2024), Getting Consumers to Pay Without a Human Interaction (2025), How to Interact with Consumers who Don't Want to Talk to a Human (2024).

By 2026 the emphasis has moved. AI vs. Human Judgment: Finding the Right Balance and AI vs. Human Collectors: Who Does it Better? both run in the first half of the year. The question shifts from removing the human toward deciding where the human still belongs.

It is a shift in emphasis, not a clean break, and I first wrote it as a clean break. Both-sides framings run throughout: The Types of Calls that Technology Can Handle and the Types that Need a Human ran in March 2022, and Artificial Intelligence-Empowering Human Agents for Better Efficiency in November 2024. The Human Oversight Imperative: When AI Needs Guardrails ran in November 2025, not 2026 as I first had it.

I went looking for a story about the phone. This one is better.

05

The catch I went looking for, and did not find

94 AI events against 94 non-AI events matched year for year

These are sponsored webinars. That is not a secret, the sponsor's logo is on the promo image. Which raises the obvious objection to everything above: if vendors bought slots to talk about AI, then a rising AI share measures marketing budget, not industry attention.

So I tested it. I pulled the promo image for every AI-themed session and for a baseline of non-AI sessions drawn from the same years in the same proportions, then read the sponsor line off each one.

Difference

+10.6pp

95% CI −0.5 to 21.8 · p = 0.064

The interval crosses zero. On the corrected sample this difference is not distinguishable from chance, and I am not claiming AI sessions are more sponsored than anything else on this calendar. I went looking for that effect because it was the obvious objection to everything above, and the data does not support it.

Getting here took three tries and each one weakened the result. The first run read 20.7 points at p = 0.0015. That was OCR running in fast mode. The second read 15.2 and then 14.3 once duplicate events came out. This one reads 10.6 at p = 0.064, and it is the first version where the sample was built with the same classifier the rest of the page uses and where a sponsored promo card was not being discarded by deduplication.

Two things are worth saying beyond the arithmetic. The confidence interval only describes sampling noise under this test's assumptions; it says nothing about the OCR misreading a label, which is a known and unfixed error here. And what survives is unremarkable: most of this calendar is sponsored, at roughly three quarters of the baseline. Sponsored programming is the normal condition, not something particular to AI.

06

The rise is not just a mix effect

Two control themes that should move if the programming mix simply shifted

A rising share can be an illusion. If an archive adds sessions in one area, everything else gets diluted and a flat theme looks like it is falling. The themes that should hold steady are the test.

Hiring, training and staffing moves within a narrow annual band across the whole period, 10.4% to 14.7%, with no direction to it. It does not get displaced by the AI rise. Compliance declines steadily but from an obvious cause: the 2021Q4 spike is Regulation F, the CFPB's debt collection rule, which took effect November 30, 2021 (85 FR 76734) and briefly consumed the entire calendar.

A flat annual control beside a climb from 0.8% to 19.8% is consistent with the AI curve being a real change in what this calendar programmed rather than an artifact of counting. It does not prove it: the staffing category overlaps AI content by keyword, and its quarterly line is much noisier than its annual one. I am also deliberately not quoting the AI change as a multiple, because the 2022 base is two sessions out of 266 and any ratio on a base that small is fragile even when the direction is not.

08

Corrections

Published 2026-09-16 morning. Corrected the same day after review.

Two rounds of independent review of the code and data found thirteen errors. All are fixed above. I am listing them rather than quietly editing, because the first version was public for several hours and a number of figures moved, including the headline.

Round two also changed the framing. The phone verdict moved from "fails" to "not established," because my classifier cannot test that proposition. With only one of four claims cleanly holding and one untestable, the original headline, "half of it was wrong," was no longer accurate and has been replaced.

Round three: the sponsorship finding is withdrawn

  • The sample was frozen against an older classifier. After I rebuilt the AI category, four records in the sample no longer qualified as AI, including Problems You Can Solve with Robotic Process Automation, and fourteen qualifying events were missing from it entirely. The statistic was describing a category the page no longer used.
  • Deduplication was throwing away sponsorship evidence. For event 221686 it kept the recording screenshot and dropped the sponsored promo card, which mechanically lowered the numerator. Dedup decides what an event is; it must not decide what evidence survives. Evidence is now unioned across every listing of an event.
  • Rebuilt properly, the effect is not there. 86.2% against 75.5%, a 10.6-point gap, 95% CI −0.5 to 21.8, p = 0.064. The interval crosses zero. Across four runs the gap went 20.7, 15.2, 14.3, 10.6, shrinking every time a measurement defect was removed. I am no longer claiming AI sessions are disproportionately sponsored.
  • I also framed the uncertainty wrongly. I wrote that the interval "nearly touches zero" as though sampling noise were the main risk. It is not. Misclassification, stale group membership and discarded evidence were the real problems, and a confidence interval does not account for any of them.
  • The staffing range was stale at 10.9% to 13.4% against regenerated data of 10.4% to 14.7%, and the voice-AI note gave a 2026 percentage next to an all-years count, which reads as though 2026 rested on 17 events. It rests on five.

Round two

  • The withdrawn voice-AI series was still being plotted. I pulled it from the verdict card but left it in the chart, legend, tooltip and table, so the page said "withdrawn" while rendering 3.8%. Rather than delete the series I rebuilt the category: it now requires an AI signal and a voice or phone signal in the same title, so AI on the Phone: The Voice (and Final) Frontier counts and How to start with Agentic AI does not. It reads 2.7% in 2026, which is five events out of 182, and seventeen across the whole archive. Too thin for a trend, and labeled as such.
  • I claimed the sponsorship gap was a lower bound. It is not. Undercounting both rates does not bound their difference in either direction. Retracted in full.
  • Six duplicate events were inflating every share. A session and its promo card can both be indexed. Deduplicating by title and date takes 1,309 dated records to 1,303 events, and one of the six pairs was sitting in both arms of the sponsorship sample. Re-run on deduplicated, re-matched samples the figures were 85.7% against 71.4%, a 14.3-point gap. Round three rebuilt the sample again and the effect did not survive.
  • Superseded sponsorship figures survived in two places. The method caveat still said 21 points, and the data README still carried the old confidence interval.
  • The chronology fix introduced a new unsupported claim. I called a November 2024 title the earliest both-sides framing. The Types of Calls that Technology Can Handle and the Types that Need a Human ran in March 2022.
  • A sensitivity note had its direction reversed. It said treating duplicates as separate sessions produces 2.1% for 2021 AI. Deduplicating produces 2.1%; treating them as separate produced 4.0%.
  • Four date-parsing defects. A length guard rejected valid short dates like "6/1/26"; a malformed year ("June 12, 20204") was silently truncated or replaced with the image's upload year; an impossible explicit date fell through to the upload year instead of being rejected; and the title parser had no calendar validation at all, so "(From 2/30/2026)" parsed. All fixed, with no change to any stored date.

Round one

  • The OCR was running in fast mode, not accurate. The Vision framework defines Accurate as 0 and Fast as 1; my code passed 1 with a comment claiming it was accurate. Fast mode misread sponsor labels, producing readings like "Spon50r:" that the match then rejected. Re-run in accurate mode, the sponsorship figures moved from 83.7% and 63.0% to 85.9% and 70.7%, and the gap from 20.7 to 15.2 points. Round two's deduplication moved them again, to the figures now shown.
  • The sponsorship section claimed more than the test could support. It said a rise from 0.8% to 20.3% "does not come from a 21-point sponsorship gap." That compares a change in programming share against a difference in prevalence between two groups, which are not the same quantity, and the comparison cannot rule out sponsorship-driven topic selection. Removed.
  • The voice-AI figure was withdrawn at 3.8%, then rebuilt in round two. See above.
  • One title was attributed to the wrong year. The Human Oversight Imperative: When AI Needs Guardrails ran 2025-11-20, and I cited it as 2026 evidence. Worse, framing the change as a clean 2026 inversion was wrong on its own terms: Artificial Intelligence-Empowering Human Agents for Better Efficiency ran in November 2024. It is a shift in emphasis, not a break, and it now reads that way.
  • "Portals sat between 1.1% and 2.2% in every year" was false. 2021 is 0 of 50. The conclusion that portals never became a theme is unaffected.
  • The control chart's screen-reader description was wrong. It said staffing "stays flat near 12 percent throughout." The annual share holds a narrow band, but the quarterly line ranges from 5.6% to 24.1%. The surrounding claim has also been softened: a flat annual control is consistent with the AI rise being real, it does not prove it.

Both issues flagged as unresolved in the first round are now fixed. Six pairs of records shared a title and date, a session and its promo card both indexed; deduplicating them takes 2021 AI from 4.0% to 2.1% and the 2024 digital peak from 9.3% to 9.4%, and every figure on this page is now event-level. The phone category is still a broad keyword bucket, so its verdict is now "not established" rather than "fails."

09

Method, and what this cannot tell you

Read before citing any figure above

  • Titles only. Session bodies are paywalled. This measures what this publication chose to program, which is a real signal about where attention went, but it is not what was said inside the room.
  • Multi-label by design. A session about texting consumers through an AI agent counts in both themes. Shares are "percent of sessions mentioning X" and never sum to 100.
  • 2021 is one quarter. The archive's first record is 2021-10-22, so 2021 is 50 sessions from October to December and its annual figures are not comparable to a full year. The 58% compliance reading is that quarter's Regulation F concentration, not a yearly rate.
  • 2026 runs to September 15, so its annual column covers three quarters.
  • One publication, not the industry. This is a single trade outlet's event calendar. It is an influential one and it programs several sessions a week, but it is one editorial viewpoint with one sponsor base. Nothing here measures what any individual agency actually does.
  • Sponsored content throughout. Roughly three quarters of this calendar carries a readable sponsor label. I tested whether AI sessions carry one more often than a year-matched baseline and could not distinguish the difference from chance. Read every share above as programming, which is part editorial judgment and part vendor spend.
  • The token trap. Filtering this archive on the word "webinar" silently drops 310 sessions, including all of 2021 and two thirds of 2023, because that token is a title convention the site changed partway through. Any trend built on the filtered set overstates growth. This analysis uses the unfiltered archive.
  • Classifier is keyword-based and was audited on random samples per theme, with "AI" matched case-sensitively as a standalone token so that email, training and available do not register as artificial intelligence.
Source. AccountsRecovery.net premium-content archive, retrieved 2026-09-16 from the site's public index. Session titles and promo images only; session recordings are behind a paywall and were not accessed.
Independence. This analysis is not affiliated with, commissioned by, or endorsed by AccountsRecovery.net or any sponsor named on the sessions counted. No sponsor is identified individually anywhere in this analysis.
Method. Keyword classification over public titles, multi-label, audited on random samples per theme. Counts and classifications are reproducible from the source index.
